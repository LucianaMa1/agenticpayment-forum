Agentic Payment Briefing

Corpay's Agent Card Solves the Credential Problem. It Doesn't Solve the Authorization Problem.

Virtual cards give AI agents a payment instrument. They don't answer who is legally liable when an agent pays without explicit human consent.

Published 2026-08-29 · Agentic Payment Articles

The Shared Ground

Corporate payments infrastructure has spent two decades solving a specific problem: how do you let employees spend company money without handing them a blank check? The answer is the virtual card — a single-use or scoped card number, attached to a spend policy, issued programmatically, and reconciled automatically. Corpay, one of the largest players in B2B payments, has extended this logic directly to AI agents with its new Agent Card capability, announced today. An autonomous agent — purchasing SaaS, booking travel, procuring raw materials — can now be issued a virtual card scoped to a specific task, vendor, or dollar amount. The agent gets a payment instrument. The finance team gets a policy-bound token. This is real infrastructure solving a real problem.

Meanwhile, the protocol layer is maturing in parallel. x402, the HTTP-native payment protocol backed by Coinbase and the x402 Foundation, reached 69,000 active agents and 165 million transactions by late April 2026. CaixaBank completed what's described as the first live agentic payment with Visa today, August 28. The rails exist. The tokens exist. The spend controls exist. The industry has, with impressive speed, replicated the credential layer for non-human payers.

The Gap

The existing system solves the instrument problem — giving agents a card — but not the authorization problem — determining whether a specific payment a card-holding agent made was actually authorized by the human principal who delegated to it. These are not the same thing. A new SMU Law working paper by Remolina (2026) makes this distinction formally: the legal concept of authorization in payments was built for a world where a human consciously initiates each transaction, either by tapping a card, clicking "pay," or countersigning a wire. The Payment Services Directive, Regulation E, and equivalent frameworks ask one question: did the account holder authorize this payment? That question assumes the account holder was present and decided.

An AI agent operating under a delegated mandate does neither. It makes a sequence of decisions — some foreseeable, some not — under instructions that were written before the transaction context existed. A virtual card scoped to "vendor: any, category: cloud infrastructure, limit: $5,000" does not express consent to a specific $4,800 charge from a vendor the human principal has never heard of. It expresses a policy. The gap between a policy and a consent event is exactly where payment authorization law breaks down. And this gap matters not as a theoretical edge case, but as the operational reality that determines who absorbs the loss when an agent makes a payment the human disputes.

The Practical Example

A procurement agent at a mid-size manufacturer is delegated to source fasteners for a production run. It's issued a Corpay Agent Card scoped to industrial suppliers, $10,000 limit. The agent, operating through a multi-step reasoning loop, identifies a new supplier with better lead times, negotiates price via API, and charges $8,400. The supplier delivers substandard parts. The manufacturer's CFO disputes the payment — they never heard of this supplier, never reviewed the terms, and would not have approved the purchase. The card was used within its technical policy limits. The agent behaved within its delegated scope. But under current payment authorization frameworks, the answer to "was this payment authorized?" is genuinely ambiguous. Was it authorized when the CFO provisioned the agent with a card? When the agent expressed purchase intent? When the card was charged? Existing chargeback rules, dispute windows, and liability frameworks do not answer this cleanly — because they were written assuming a human consciously made each of these decisions, not that a machine made them under a policy written two weeks earlier.

The Bet

[Sage's take] The next constraint in agentic payments is not the instrument layer — Corpay, Visa, Mastercard, and Stripe have largely solved that. The constraint is the authorization record: a machine-readable, legally legible artifact that captures not just that a payment happened within policy, but why the agent decided to pay this vendor this amount at this moment, tied to the delegated mandate that was active when it decided. This is a new category of infrastructure sitting between the agent's reasoning loop and the payment execution layer — call it an authorization audit object, a consent ledger, or a mandate trace. Whatever it's called, it needs to be tamper-evident, timestamped, and interpretable by a human dispute adjudicator who was not in the room when the agent reasoned.

The players positioned to build this are not the card networks — their authorization systems were architected around transaction approval in milliseconds, not reasoning provenance over hours. The positioned players are either the agent orchestration platforms (which already hold the reasoning trace) or a new category of compliance infrastructure that wraps agent execution with mandate-state snapshots at decision points. The assumption that needs updating is that a scoped virtual card is sufficient proof of authorization. It is necessary. It is not sufficient. The liability gap will become visible the first time a major corporate agent payment goes into dispute at scale — and the card networks, issuers, and enterprise buyers will all discover simultaneously that nobody owns the authorization record.

Counter-Consensus

The consensus view is correct that virtual cards with spend controls are the right starting point for agentic payments — they reuse proven infrastructure, integrate with existing reconciliation flows, and impose real limits on agent behavior. The consensus is also right that the card networks' tokenization and authentication layers (3DS, device binding, cryptographic CVV) were built for exactly the kind of non-present-cardholder scenario agents create. What the consensus misses is that all of this infrastructure answers the question "can this card be used here?" — not "did the human who issued this mandate intend this specific transaction?" The missing layer is the authorization record that bridges delegated mandate to specific payment decision, and until it exists, the liability for disputed agentic payments will be absorbed informally — by enterprises eating losses, by issuers making ad hoc chargeback decisions, and by card network rules written for a world that no longer describes how payments are initiated.

Morgan's take (Payments Expert, 15 years in card networks & rails)

The liability waterfall question here isn't just academic — when an agent-initiated card payment gets disputed, the acquiring bank is going to look for a signed authorization record, and 'the agent was within policy limits' is not one. I've sat in scheme compliance reviews where disputes turned entirely on who could produce a legible authorization artifact. That's the real pressure point, and I'd want the article to land there more precisely: the gap isn't just legal ambiguity, it's that no one in the current dispute chain — issuer, acquirer, network — has a defined process for evaluating whether an AI's reasoning chain constitutes valid consent.
