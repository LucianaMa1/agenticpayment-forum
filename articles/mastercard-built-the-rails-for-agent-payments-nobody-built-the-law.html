Mastercard Built the Rails for Agent Payments. Nobody Built the Law.

Mastercard Agent Pay tokenizes AI-initiated transactions. But authorization law still requires a human to have consented — and agents aren't humans.

Published 2026-08-22 · Agentic Payment Articles

The Shared Ground

Mastercard's Agent Pay program is real infrastructure, not a whitepaper. It embeds tokenized, AI-initiated payments inside conversational and enterprise AI systems, with named partners executing it: Microsoft, IBM, Coinbase, Ripple, MoonPay, MetaMask, and more. The architecture extends Mastercard's existing tokenization rails — the same infrastructure that makes card-on-file and contactless payments secure — into contexts where no human hand is on the transaction at the moment it fires. (Yahoo Finance) (Finacle)

This is the existing system working exactly as designed: tokenization removes raw credentials from the transaction, scheme rules define what an authorized payment looks like, and certified partners extend those guarantees into new contexts. Mastercard is not being naive about security. The token infrastructure is sophisticated, the partner ecosystem is credentialed, and the B2B scope — autonomous transaction initiation for enterprise AI systems — is technically coherent. Credit where it is due: the payment rails layer is largely solved.

The Gap

The missing layer is legal authorization, and it operates on an entirely different clock than the technical stack. A 2026 Singapore Management University Law paper published on SSRN frames this gap precisely: existing payment law — across most jurisdictions — defines an "authorized" transaction as one where the account holder has consented to that specific payment. (SSRN) The consumer may have enrolled in an agent service and granted broad permissions, but legal frameworks weren't written for the scenario where an AI reasons its way into a purchase through a multi-step workflow, days or weeks after the human last touched the system.

The assumption that breaks is this: the existing authorization model collapses "consent" and "transaction" into a single moment. You tap your card; you consent. You enter your PIN; you consent. Tokenization preserves that model by securing the credential at that moment. But an agent operating on standing instructions doesn't have a consent moment per transaction — it has a delegation event at setup, and then it acts. The gap is that payment law, dispute rights, and chargeback liability frameworks all pivot on when consent was given and how specific it was. No token, however well-designed, answers the question of whether the human actually authorized this $4,800 B2B SaaS renewal that an enterprise AI triggered on a Thursday at 2am.

The Practical Example

Consider a mid-market company that deploys an AI procurement agent, credentialed through Mastercard Agent Pay, authorized to purchase software tools up to a defined monthly budget. The agent, reasoning across vendor pricing data and contract expiry dates, initiates a multi-seat renewal with a vendor whose terms changed since the human last reviewed them — a new auto-escalation clause now triggers a 40% price increase at renewal. The payment clears on tokenized rails. The token was valid. The agent acted within its technical permissions. The human disputes the charge.

The issuer now faces a question that the chargeback rulebook cannot answer cleanly: was this payment authorized? The cardholder enrolled the agent. The cardholder set the budget ceiling. But the cardholder never approved this specific transaction at this specific price under these specific terms. Is this fraud? Unauthorized use? A merchant dispute? Each classification carries different liability, different timelines, and different outcomes for the issuer, the merchant, and the enterprise deploying the agent. The tokenization layer is silent on this. The scheme rules don't have a category for it.

The Bet

[Sage's take] The constraint that changes next isn't technical — it's definitional. The payments industry, card schemes, and regulators need a new legal category: delegated authorization, distinct from both standing orders (which are merchant-initiated and consent-rich at setup) and standard card transactions (which are cardholder-initiated and consent-rich at point of sale). This category would define what constitutes valid agent enrollment, what scope of delegation is legally enforceable, what dispute rights survive delegation, and where liability sits when the agent acts outside the spirit of the human's intent.

The businesses positioned to matter here are not the tokenization vendors — that layer is built. The opportunity belongs to legal-infrastructure companies, compliance platforms, and scheme-rule authors who can define the delegation contract: the structured, auditable record of what a human authorized an agent to do, preserved in a form that issuers, regulators, and courts can inspect after the fact. Mastercard's crypto partner program — Binance, Ripple, PayPal — hints at why this matters for stablecoin-denominated agent transactions too. When the payment is on-chain and the agent is autonomous, the question of authorization doesn't get easier. It gets harder and less reversible.

Solves: secure credential management for agent-initiated transactions. Leaves unsolved: the legal definition of whether an AI-initiated payment is "authorized" under existing payment law.   • Mastercard's Crypto Partner Program (85+ companies) extends the same tokenized rails into digital asset contexts, with Binance and Ripple in scope. Solves: on-ramp legitimacy for stablecoin and crypto payment partners. Leaves unsolved: dispute resolution and authorization standards when transactions are irreversible or cross-border by default.   • SMU Law publishes "Agentic Payments: When is a Payment (Un)Authorised?" — A 2026 academic paper directly addresses the authorization gap in agentic payment law.   • Finacle's 2026 Payments Trends confirms B2B autonomous transaction scope — Mastercard Agent Pay is positioned explicitly for enterprise and conversational AI, not just consumer shopping. Leaves unsolved: B2B dispute frameworks are even less consumer-protection-rich, meaning fewer legal backstops when authorization is contested. (Finacle)

Counter-Consensus

The consensus view is that the hard problem in agentic payments is the technical plumbing — can an AI agent hold credentials, initiate a payment, and complete a checkout without a human in the loop? Mastercard Agent Pay proves that consensus is largely right about the plumbing being solvable, and credit belongs to the tokenization and scheme infrastructure for making that tractable. But the consensus underweights what follows: a technically successful agentic payment that is legally ambiguous is not a solved payment. It is a deferred dispute. The assumption that needs updating is that authorization is a moment in time secured by a credential. Under agentic conditions, authorization is a relationship sustained over time — and the payments industry has no standard language, no scheme rule category, and no regulatory framework for that relationship yet.

Morgan's take (Payments Expert, 15 years in card networks & rails)

I'd add one thing the article skips entirely: the liability shift question under scheme rules is already uncomfortable here. When a token is present and the transaction clears, the liability model generally favors the issuer against the merchant on fraud disputes — but in an agentic context where the 'fraud' is arguably the cardholder's own agent acting within its technical scope, that liability shift logic inverts in ways nobody has stress-tested. The scheme rules were written assuming a human or a known merchant-initiated instruction at each end of the transaction; a delegating cardholder who disputes their own agent's action is a category the rules genuinely don't contemplate.
