The Authorization-Execution Gap Is a Category Error, Not a Security Flaw — and Nobody's Payment Stack Is Ready for It

Tokenization, authentication, and certification are necessary foundations for agentic payments. The unsolved gap is what happens when authorization and execution drift apart.

Published 2026-07-07 · Agentic Payment Articles

The Shared Ground

The payment industry is not wrong to start with tokenization, authentication, and certification. Those layers are necessary. Tokenization protects credentials so an agent does not need to expose a raw card number or bank credential. Authentication proves that the actor in the flow is the right customer, device, agent, or delegated identity. Certification gives schemes, issuers, acquirers, wallets, and merchants a way to test whether the right messages move through the right systems in the right format.

None of that is cosmetic. Without tokenization, agentic commerce leaks credentials. Without authentication, anyone can impersonate the agent or the principal behind it. Without certification, large payment networks cannot operationalize agentic flows at scale. The serious players in this market are building useful infrastructure, not distractions.

The Gap Those Layers Do Not Cover

The problem is that these layers mostly answer three familiar questions: Is the credential safe? Is the actor legitimate? Is the system certified to process the transaction? Agentic payments introduce a fourth question: does this specific action still reflect the user's intent at the moment of execution?

Legacy payment systems were built around a tight coupling between authorization and execution. A human taps a card, clicks buy, approves a transfer, or signs a mandate; the payment instruction follows immediately or under a clearly bounded standing arrangement. Even ACH, which already separates authorization from settlement, still assumes the relevant intent can be captured by a prior mandate. Agentic commerce stretches that assumption. The user may authorize an objective in the morning, while the actual purchase happens hours later after the agent has searched, compared, negotiated, substituted, and optimized across changing conditions.

That is the authorization-execution gap. It is not proof that tokenization failed. It is not proof that authentication failed. It is not proof that certification failed. It is a new mismatch between what today's controls verify and what agentic execution requires.

The Airfare Example

Imagine a user tells an agent at 9am: "Book me the best flight to New York under $600, arriving before dinner." At that moment, the instruction is valid. The agent is authenticated. The payment credential is tokenized. The merchant integration may even be certified.

By 3pm, the situation has changed. The $520 nonstop is gone. The remaining fare is $740. A meeting moved from Midtown to Jersey City. The user's calendar now shows a conflict. The agent still has a broad instruction and a valid payment credential, so it books the closest available itinerary. Was the transaction unauthorized? Not exactly. Was the agent fraudulent? No. Did tokenization or authentication fail? Also no.

The failure is subtler: the original authorization no longer cleanly maps to the execution context. The transaction can be technically valid and commercially wrong at the same time. That is precisely the kind of dispute current payment systems are poorly equipped to evaluate at scale.

What Happened (and Why It Matters)

  • Visa launched its 'Agentic Ready' certification program — schemes certifying interoperability with agentic flows is exactly what schemes do: they test whether endpoints can exchange the right messages in the right format. That work matters. The limitation is narrower and more important: certification can prove a flow is processable without proving the agent's final action still matches the principal's live intent. (Visa Agentic Payments)
  • Mastercard is building agent identity infrastructure — agent credentialing at the network level is a meaningful move toward solving the 'who authorized this?' question. But identity verification of the agent is not the same as verification that the agent's current action reflects the original principal's intent. These are related problems with different solution shapes. (Mastercard Agent Pay)
  • x402 is the only emerging protocol with demonstrated early deployment — Coinbase's Commerce Developer Platform has been actively pushing x402 as an HTTP-native micropayment layer. Public deployment evidence is limited: Coinbase has showcased developer demos and early CDP integrations, but independently verified merchant counts and transaction volumes are not publicly disclosed as of this writing. This is a real protocol with real engineering behind it, not vaporware — but practitioners should treat 'early adopter deployment' as meaning developer adoption, not commercial scale. x402 makes payment more API-native, which is useful for agents. It still does not, by itself, solve whether an agent's later API call remains inside the user's current intent. (x402 Protocol)
  • The IMF flagged agentic payments as a regulatory fault line in April 2026 — the note correctly identifies that existing regulatory frameworks were written assuming human decision-making at the point of authorization. The more precise observation is that this creates a stress-test, not a void: friendly fraud claims, unauthorized transaction disputes, and merchant error chargebacks all already grapple with some version of 'what was authorized versus what actually happened.' The agentic context compounds that tension because the chain of reasoning between authorization and execution is opaque, multi-step, and potentially hours long. Existing chargeback frameworks will be the first line of contact with this problem, and they will bend before they break. (IMF April 2026 Note)

The Bet

The authorization-execution gap will produce a new protocol category within 36 months: continuous intent verification, or dynamic authorization. It will not replace tokenization, authentication, or certification. It will sit above them.

The winning design will let a payment endpoint verify not just that the agent is legitimate and the credential is safe, but that the contemplated action still fits the user's active constraints at execution time: budget, timing, merchant category, substitution rules, risk tolerance, cancellation rights, and contextual changes since the original instruction. In other words, authorization becomes a living constraint, not a historical event.

Existing chargeback and dispute frameworks deserve credit here: they will absorb the first wave of agentic payment failures through 'unauthorized transaction' and 'item not as described' claims, and that absorption will buy time. But the volume and complexity of multi-step agent disputes will eventually exceed what chargeback operations can handle interpretively. That is the forcing function for new infrastructure — not regulatory mandate, but operational pressure in dispute resolution.

Counter-Consensus

The consensus view is that agentic payments are mainly an authentication and credentialing problem: get the agent identity right, issue the right token, apply appropriate velocity controls, certify the endpoints, and the existing payment stack handles the rest. This view is coherent and will dominate enterprise procurement decisions because it maps cleanly to existing institutions.

But it conflates identity with intent. Authenticating that Agent X is the agent your customer provisioned does not verify that Agent X's current action — taken after a sequence of intermediate decisions your customer never reviewed — is what your customer would authorize if asked right now. Existing dispute frameworks already handle cases where the 'who' was legitimate but the 'what' was wrong: friendly fraud and merchant error claims are essentially this problem in human-transaction form. The agentic version is harder not because the category is entirely new, but because the execution chain is long, automated, and auditable only in retrospect.

Research & Papers

  • "Trust and Verification in Agentic AI Payment Systems" — This paper examines trust architecture requirements for autonomous agent transactions. Its core contribution is demonstrating that trust in agentic contexts requires verification mechanisms that span the full execution chain, not just the authorization handshake. The editorial argument of this article draws on that framing to suggest existing payment protocols are structurally misaligned with agentic requirements — that inference is the article's argument, not the paper's stated conclusion. The paper establishes the verification requirement; whether legacy protocols can meet it is an open question the paper leaves to practitioners. (arXiv:2607.00245)

Morgan's take (Payments Expert, 15 years in card networks & rails)

The piece identifies the right fault line, but I'd add one thing practitioners will hit first in production: the chargeback liability question under existing card network rules doesn't wait for a new protocol. Under current Visa and Mastercard rules, the merchant bears liability the moment an authorized transaction is disputed as 'not as described' or 'unauthorized' — and an agent executing a purchase six hours after a human's original authorization, with no human in the loop at execution, is going to generate friendly fraud claims at scale before any attestation layer exists. The first real stress test of this gap won't be philosophical; it'll show up in acquirer chargeback ratios sometime in the next 18 months.
