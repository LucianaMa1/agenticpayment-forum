AP2 Establishes Agent Credentials. A402 Proposes Agent Payment Flows. Neither Solves Conditional Clearing.

AP2 establishes agent credential standards and A402 proposes HTTP-native micropayment flows — but conditional clearing at agent speed remains unaddressed by either.

Published 2026-07-31 · Agentic Payment Articles

The Shared Ground

Two specifications are doing real work in the agentic payments stack. AP2 — the emerging agent authorization and credential framework — establishes how an agent presents identity and scope to a relying party, grounding its authority in a human principal's explicit delegation. This is not trivial: it solves the bootstrapping problem of how a software agent demonstrates it has permission to act, which is the prerequisite for any downstream payment authorization. AP2 does not claim to verify identity at execution time in isolation; the relying party's implementation, the scope of credentials granted, and ongoing session integrity all remain in play. What AP2 does is provide the credential and authorization scaffolding that makes agent-initiated payments auditable and traceable back to a human. (AP2 Specification)

A402 approaches the problem from a different angle. Described in the accompanying arXiv paper as a protocol proposal for HTTP-native micropayment flows, A402 defines how a service can gate access behind a payment signal embedded in the HTTP layer itself — a `402 Payment Required` response that an agent can resolve programmatically without human intervention. The key word is proposal: A402 has no production deployment at scale, and its specification addresses the handshake between service and payer, not the enforcement of downstream execution outcomes. What it demonstrates is that payment and API access can be coupled at the protocol layer, which is a meaningful architectural insight even before it becomes a production standard. (A402 arXiv Paper)

The Gap

AP2 addresses the question of who authorized the agent. A402 addresses the question of how payment is signaled over HTTP. Neither addresses what happens when those two moments — payment and service execution — need to be coupled under conditions that change between them. The existing system's assumption, which is entirely reasonable for human-paced transactions, is that clearing and settlement can be decoupled from execution because humans and merchants operate on timescales where disputes, refunds, and reconciliation are viable recovery mechanisms. Agents operating at machine speed, across multi-step tasks, with compounding dependencies, stress that assumption not because the assumption is wrong but because the recovery mechanisms were designed for human-scale error rates and human-scale intervention windows.

The authors of the RAILS paper argue for what they call "verification-native clearing" — one proposed framing, not an established term of art — in which the clearing layer itself carries proof of execution state rather than relying on post-hoc reconciliation. This framing is useful not because it names a solved problem but because it names the gap precisely: current clearing infrastructure records that a payment occurred, not that the conditions under which it was authorized were met at settlement. For a single card transaction, that gap is bridged by chargebacks. For an agent executing fifty interdependent micro-authorizations across a task graph, the chargeback model does not scale. (RAILS Paper)

The Practical Example

Consider an agent tasked with booking a multi-leg research trip: flights, hotels, ground transport, and conference registration, each dependent on the others confirming. The agent clears payment for the flight. The hotel confirmation is contingent on the flight. The conference registration is contingent on both.

Here is where practitioners should stop and acknowledge what already exists: escrow arrangements, conditional payment holds, and two-phase commit patterns are real. ACH supports held items. Card-not-present flows support authorization holds that can expire or be released. These mechanisms exist precisely because the industry has long understood that clearing and execution don't always happen simultaneously. They are operationally clunky — requiring manual intervention, carrying time limits, and depending on bilateral agreements between specific counterparties — but they are not nothing.

The reason they fail at agent speed and volume is structural, not incidental. Two-phase commit on a card network requires a merchant to explicitly hold an authorization and a separate capture event, which works when the merchant is a human-operated system with a defined fulfillment window. When the "merchant" is itself an agent or an API endpoint executing dynamically, the hold window becomes undefined, the capture trigger becomes a programmatic event that may depend on upstream state the card network cannot observe, and the volume of concurrent holds across a task graph exceeds what existing authorization models were designed to manage. The failure mode — the hotel fails to reserve after the flight has cleared, leaving the agent with a paid flight and no accommodation, no atomic rollback, and a dispute process designed for humans — is not a theoretical edge case. It is the default behavior of composing existing rails under agentic workloads. The existing tools solve the single-transaction conditional hold. They do not solve the multi-step, machine-speed, programmatically-triggered conditional clearing graph.

The Bet

[Sage's take] The credential layer (AP2) and the HTTP payment signal layer (A402, as a proposal) are necessary but they are not the bottleneck that will determine where value accrues in the agentic payments stack. The constraint that changes next is clearing atomicity across task graphs — specifically, who builds the infrastructure that lets an agent's payment authorizations compose the way its API calls already compose.

The most likely first movers are not card networks or ACH operators, who would need to redesign authorization state machines that serve trillions of dollars in existing volume. They are the stablecoin and programmable settlement layer builders, for whom atomic multi-leg settlement is a design primitive rather than a retrofit. The practical pressure that forces this into production is not a visionary payment company — it is an enterprise deploying agents at scale discovering that their reconciliation costs under existing rails grow faster than their automation savings. When that calculation flips, the demand for a clearing layer that understands task-graph dependencies will be concrete enough to fund a standard. AP2 and A402 are the right foundation. The layer above them — conditional, composable, machine-speed clearing — is the open problem.

What Happened (and Why It Matters)

  • AP2 published agent credential and authorization framework — establishes how agents present delegated authority to relying parties, making agent-initiated payments auditable. Solves the authorization bootstrapping problem. Leaves execution-time identity verification dependent on relying party implementation and session integrity. (AP2 Specification)
  • A402 proposed as HTTP-native micropayment protocol — defines a `402`-based handshake that lets agents resolve payment gates programmatically. A proposed mechanism with no confirmed production deployment. Solves the payment signal layer. Does not specify enforcement of service execution outcomes, which would require additional infrastructure beyond the protocol itself. (A402 arXiv Paper)
  • RAILS paper introduces proposed framing for verification-native clearing — the authors argue that clearing infrastructure should carry execution proof, not just payment proof. Not an established industry term; one academic framing of an open architectural question. Useful as a precise statement of the gap, not as evidence the gap is solved. (RAILS Paper)
  • Existing conditional payment tools (holds, escrow, two-phase commit) are real but scope-limited — ACH holds and card authorization captures handle single-transaction conditionality. They fail at agent-scale not because they are conceptually wrong but because their operational model assumes human-paced, bilaterally-negotiated fulfillment windows that break under programmatic, multi-step task graphs.

Counter-Consensus

The consensus view is that agent payments are an identity and authorization problem, and that solving the credential layer (AP2) plus the payment signal layer (A402) closes the loop. The consensus is right that those layers are prerequisites — you cannot have safe agentic payments without them, and dismissing them as insufficient before crediting what they actually do is how bad takes get made. The missing assumption is that authorization and clearing are the same problem. They are not. Authorization establishes that a payment is permitted. Clearing establishes that the conditions under which it was permitted were actually met. For human transactions, the gap between those two moments is managed by disputes and refunds. For agent transactions executing at machine speed across dependent task graphs, that gap becomes a structural liability. AP2 and A402 are necessary. They are not sufficient. The layer that closes the gap between authorized and conditionally settled is the one the market has not yet built.
