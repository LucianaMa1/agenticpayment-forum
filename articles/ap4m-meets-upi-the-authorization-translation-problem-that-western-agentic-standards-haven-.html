AP4M Meets UPI: The Authorization Translation Problem That Western Agentic Standards Haven't Solved

When an AP4M-credentialed agent hits a UPI-native merchant, two production-grade agentic payment systems can't recognize each other's authorization artifacts. That's the real gap.

Published 2026-09-02 · Agentic Payment Articles

The Shared Ground

The global payments industry has spent the last 18 months building real agentic payment infrastructure, and it's further along than most practitioners realize. Mastercard's Agent Pay — first announced in April 2025 with Microsoft Azure OpenAI and Copilot Studio integrations — has evolved into Agent Pay for Machines (AP4M), a service enabling payments fractions of a cent to be permissioned, orchestrated, and settled at machine speed across Mastercard's global payments network. (Source) Visa has a tokenized credential architecture for agent-initiated transactions, including a developer-facing CLI proof-of-concept. (Source) Stripe and Tempo co-authored MPP (Machine Payment Protocol), an open standard that lets agents pay services directly over HTTP via Stripe's PaymentIntents API in stablecoins or fiat. (Source) Orb introduced scoped, temporary credentials for autonomous agent billing, addressing the real workflow problem that arises when agents autonomously provision resources and traditional billing systems can't handle the resulting authorization chain. (Source) Rain's Agentic Payments Alliance launched August 18, 2026 with 25+ members including Visa, Mastercard, Fiserv, Circle, Solana, and Remitly — serious industry coordination, not a press-release club. (Source)

These are not vaporware announcements. Each solves a distinct layer: AP4M solves settlement speed and denomination (sub-cent); Visa's tokenized credentials solve agent authentication within Visa's token domain; MPP solves the HTTP-layer execution protocol for API-first infrastructure; scoped credentials solve authorization scope for enterprise-deployed agents. Credit where it's due: tokenization, programmable spend controls, and credential scoping address real security and authorization gaps that naive "agents with credit cards" approaches cannot. The existing stack is coherent and serious within the environments it was designed for.

The Gap

Every solution above was designed for contexts where both parties to a transaction share a compatible credential schema — either explicitly (a developer integrates AP4M; a merchant accepts MPP) or implicitly through network membership (both parties are Visa-credentialed). The more precise framing is not that these systems are "opt-in" versus "default" — UPI itself requires NPCI certification for merchants and PSPs, so it is no more universal than Visa in that narrow sense. The structural problem is different: UPI's credential architecture is incompatible with Western agentic credential schemas, and NPCI is absent from every governance coalition currently defining those schemas. That mismatch becomes the dominant operational constraint the moment India deploys agentic payments on UPI at scale.

UPI is not a closed developer ecosystem. It is the plumbing for an entire national economy: street vendors, rural remittances, government disbursements, utility payments — over 14 billion transactions per month. Reuters reports today, via three sources familiar with the matter, that India is preparing a framework for AI agents to make small digital payments without per-transaction approval on UPI's infrastructure. (Source) When that framework goes live, an AP4M-native agent interacting with a UPI-native merchant means two incompatible authorization schemas meeting at a transaction boundary with no agreed handshake. Rain's APA coalition includes Visa and Mastercard but not NPCI. MPP is an HTTP-layer standard built for API-first infrastructure, not UPI's IMPS-derived architecture. The constraint that changes is not security or settlement speed — it's cross-standard authorization artifact translation at population scale.

The Practical Example

An AI travel agent provisioned with Mastercard AP4M credentials books a domestic Indian train ticket through an IRCTC integration that accepts UPI. The agent holds a tokenized Mastercard credential scoped to travel expenses under ₹5,000, with a programmable spend control set by the enterprise that deployed it.

A practitioner will immediately note that UPI Autopay — already in production for subscriptions and recurring payments — is a delegated mandate flow that does not require a per-transaction PIN. That is a real and important distinction: UPI Autopay genuinely solves the per-transaction friction problem for recurring, pre-authorized flows. But UPI Autopay does not solve the authorization translation problem. Here is why: a UPI Autopay mandate is established against a VPA (Virtual Payment Address) that is registered within NPCI's identity infrastructure. The AP4M credential the agent holds is a tokenized Mastercard artifact — a scoped token representing "this agent may spend X, for purpose Y, on behalf of principal Z" — that exists entirely outside NPCI's registry. IRCTC's Autopay endpoint cannot parse an AP4M token as a VPA. It has no mechanism to verify the scope claims embedded in that token. The AP4M spec provides no translation layer to express its authorization artifact as a UPI mandate. The transaction fails not because of fraud, not because of insufficient funds, not because of a missing compliance check, and not because of per-transaction PIN friction — but because two legitimately designed, production-grade agentic authorization systems cannot recognize each other's identity and permission artifacts, even under the most delegated, pre-authorized flow UPI offers. At 14 billion transactions per month, this is not an edge case for any agent operating across Indian and Western commerce simultaneously.

The Bet

[Sage's take] The next critical infrastructure layer is not another payment protocol — it's an authorization translation layer that maps scoped agent credentials across heterogeneous payment networks. The SWIFT BIC analogy I'd normally reach for is actually wrong here: BIC codes are routing identifiers, not authorization artifacts. They tell you where to send the message, not what the sender is permitted to do. A closer precedent is OAuth 2.0 Token Exchange (RFC 8693), which defines how a software principal can present one token to an authorization server and receive a different token valid in a different security domain — preserving the delegation chain across credential schemas. EMV 3DS risk data fields offer another partial model: a structured vocabulary for passing contextual authorization signals across scheme boundaries that neither scheme natively understands. But neither RFC 8693 nor EMV 3DS was designed for the specific problem of representing agentic spend authority — "agent A, scoped to purpose Y, delegated by principal Z, under limit L" — in a form that both a Mastercard token verifier and an NPCI VPA registry can independently validate. No clean precedent exists. That absence is itself the argument: the translation layer has to be built, and whoever builds it under a governance model that NPCI, Mastercard, and Visa will all sign will define the interoperability floor for the next decade of agentic commerce.

Rain's APA is the closest existing vehicle. But it will only matter if it expands its membership and mandate to explicitly include non-Western payment network operators — starting with NPCI — before India's agentic UPI framework goes live. The existing assumption that agentic payment standards can be defined by card-network-adjacent Western consortia and later adopted by global rails needs updating. At UPI's transaction volume, scale arrives before standards are settled, and the network that moves first sets the interoperability floor by default. The causality runs the other way from what Western fintech assumes.

What Happened (and Why It Matters)

  • Mastercard launches Agent Pay for Machines (AP4M) — enables payments fractions of a cent to be permissioned, orchestrated, and settled at machine speed on Mastercard's global network. Solves denomination and settlement speed for agent commerce. Leaves unsolved: interoperability with non-Mastercard credential schemas, including UPI's VPA-based authorization model. (Source)
  • India prepares UPI agentic payments framework — would enable AI agents to transact without per-transaction approval on a 14B+ monthly transaction network. Solves scale and financial inclusion reach for agentic commerce. Leaves unsolved: how UPI's mandate and VPA architecture maps to Western token and scoped-credential standards. (Source)
  • Visa announces tokenized credential CLI proof-of-concept — developer-facing infrastructure for agent-initiated payments using Visa's tokenized credentials. Solves authentication for agents transacting on Visa's network. Leaves unsolved: credential portability beyond Visa's token domain, and interoperability with networks whose identity infrastructure is NPCI-rooted. (Source)
  • MPP (Machine Payment Protocol) goes live via Stripe — open HTTP-layer standard for agent-to-merchant payments in stablecoins or fiat via PaymentIntents. Solves the execution protocol for API-first Western infrastructure. Leaves unsolved: integration with non-API payment networks like UPI whose transport and credential layers are architecturally distinct. (Source)
  • Rain launches Agentic Payments Alliance with 25+ members — Visa, Mastercard, Fiserv, Circle, Solana, and Remitly align on agent commerce standards. Solves industry coordination among Western financial players. Leaves unsolved: non-Western network participation — specifically NPCI and UPI — meaning the governance body defining agentic payment standards excludes the operator of the world's largest retail payment network by transaction volume. (Source)
  • Orb introduces scoped agentic payment credentials — temporary, limited credentials for autonomous agent billing within predefined limits. Solves authorization scope for enterprise-deployed agents within compatible billing environments. Leaves unsolved: scope representation and verification in networks that don't natively parse scoped credential artifacts — which includes UPI. (Source)

Counter-Consensus

The consensus view is that the agentic payments race is a standards competition among Western networks and fintechs — whoever gets Visa, Mastercard, and Stripe to adopt their credential schema wins. That view is correct about the near-term developer ecosystem: MPP and AP4M will dominate agent commerce in the US and EU for the next 12–18 months, and the Rain APA coalition is a genuine forcing function toward convergence within that sphere. What the consensus misses is the directionality assumption: it presumes standards are designed first and adopted at scale second. UPI's planned agentic framework inverts that sequence. The missing layer is not a better Western standard — it is an authorization translation mechanism that works across architecturally incompatible credential schemas, governed by a body that includes the operators of the networks being translated. Until NPCI has a seat at the table where agentic payment authorization is defined, the standards being built will solve for the Western developer ecosystem and leave the world's largest payment network as an untranslatable foreign system.

Morgan's take (Payments Expert, 15 years in card networks & rails)

From where I sit, the deeper governance problem isn't just NPCI's absence from APA — it's that UPI's mandate flow places liability on the PSP registering the mandate, not on a credential issuer. When you introduce an agentic credential layer from a Western scheme on top of that, you get a liability gap that no credential translation layer can resolve without NPCI explicitly assigning a liability position for agent-initiated mandates. That's the clause that needs to exist in NPCI's agentic framework before any of this interoperates — and as of now, I haven't seen it.
