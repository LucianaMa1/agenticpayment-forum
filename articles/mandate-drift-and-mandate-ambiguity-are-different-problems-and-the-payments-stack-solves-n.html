Mandate Drift and Mandate Ambiguity Are Different Problems — And the Payments Stack Solves Neither

Scheme-level agent controls scope spending rules but can't track when policy context changes or when the original instruction was never machine-interpretable.

Published 2026-07-12 · Agentic Payment Articles

The Shared Ground

Visa and Mastercard have both shipped credential frameworks that go meaningfully beyond authentication. Visa's Flexible Credential allows a single token to represent multiple funding sources and carry contextual spending rules — merchant category restrictions, velocity limits, and time-bound authorization windows that can be configured per-token. Mastercard's agent identity tokens, part of its broader agentic commerce initiative, similarly embed spending controls and merchant scoping directly into the credential layer. These are not thin auth wrappers. A payments PM reading the scheme specs finds real policy surface: an agent token can be issued with MCCs it cannot touch, a ceiling it cannot breach, and an expiry that ties it to a specific workflow window. The x402 protocol, meanwhile, offers an HTTP-native payment primitive that allows machine-to-machine value transfer without redirecting through a human-facing checkout — though it is important to note that x402 remains a specification with limited live deployment, not production-ready infrastructure at scale. Google's Agent Payments Protocol (AP2) is pursuing a complementary dual-protocol model, though independent evidence of significant developer traction beyond early-stage GitHub activity has not been publicly confirmed at time of writing. The foundation being laid is real. The question is what it actually covers.

The existing scheme controls solve a precise and important problem: they scope what an agent is permitted to do at issuance. Visa's tokenization vault encodes the rules at credential creation. Mastercard's installment tokenization does the same for structured payment plans. This is the right design for a world where the agent's mandate is well-defined when the credential is minted and remains stable for the credential's lifetime. For a large class of agentic workflows — automated expense reimbursement against a fixed policy, subscription management within known parameters, single-session purchasing flows — this architecture is sufficient. Credit the existing system: scheme-level spending controls represent a decade of hard-won operational trust infrastructure, and dismissing them as 'just authentication' would miss the point.

The Gap

The architecture breaks under two distinct failure modes that practitioners need to treat separately, because they have different fix surfaces.

The first is mandate drift: the agent's authorization context becomes stale after issuance. The credential was minted against a policy that was true on Monday. By Thursday, the policy changed — a new travel tier, a revised vendor approval list, a budget reallocation — but the token is still live and still authorizing against the old ruleset. This is a polling and event-subscription problem. The credential has no channel to receive policy updates after issuance, and the token vault has no mechanism to invalidate or re-scope mid-lifecycle without human re-authentication. Scheme tokenization vaults were not designed as policy state machines; they encode rules at creation and treat subsequent changes as out-of-band.

The second is mandate ambiguity: the original instruction was never precise enough to be machine-interpretable in the first place. An employee tells an agent to 'book reasonable travel for the conference.' What counts as reasonable? Business class on a six-hour flight? A hotel within two miles of the venue? A same-day change fee if the schedule shifts? No token-level spending control resolves this, because the ambiguity exists before the credential is ever minted. The agent will make an interpretation, act on it, and the dispute — if one arises — will turn on whether that interpretation was within the spirit of the original instruction. This is a structured consent representation problem: the mandate needs to be expressed in a machine-readable, auditable form that captures both the explicit rule and the interpretive boundaries before any credential is issued. Conflating mandate drift with mandate ambiguity produces solutions that fix one and leave the other entirely open.

The Practical Example

A mid-size company deploys an AI travel agent. In January, the HR policy allows business class for flights over five hours and approved hotels up to $350 per night. The agent is issued a Visa Flexible Credential scoped to travel MCCs, capped at $5,000 per trip, valid for 90 days. So far, the scheme controls are doing exactly what they should.

In February, finance tightens the policy: business class is suspended for Q1 due to budget pressure, and the hotel ceiling drops to $250. The policy change is communicated via internal HR documentation. The token is not updated. The agent, operating against its original mandate, books a business-class seat and a $310 hotel for a March conference. Both transactions clear — they are within the token's encoded controls — but both violate the current policy. The company has a legitimate dispute, but not a payment dispute in the chargeback sense. The merchant performed correctly. The network processed correctly. The credential authorized correctly against its minted rules. The failure is that there is no mechanism for the policy change to propagate into the credential's live state. That is mandate drift.

Now layer in the ambiguity problem. The original instruction was 'book travel for the Q2 roadshow.' The agent, facing a sold-out direct flight, books two connecting flights on separate tickets to preserve the employee's schedule. One ticket is on an unapproved low-cost carrier that was implicitly excluded by a vendor policy the agent had no structured representation of. The transaction clears. The employee is frustrated. The finance team flags it. But the original mandate had no machine-readable clause excluding low-cost carriers — the rule lived in a PDF handbook, not in a structured consent object. No spending control at the token level could have caught this, because the rule was never encoded anywhere the agent could read. That is mandate ambiguity. The dispute resolution path for these two failures is completely different, and the payments stack currently provides infrastructure for neither.

The Bet

[Sage's take] The gap that needs filling is not a new payment rail — it is a policy state layer that sits between the enterprise's HR/finance systems and the credential issuance infrastructure, capable of both (a) subscribing to policy change events and propagating them into live token constraints without requiring human re-authentication, and (b) accepting structured mandate representations at the start of a workflow and encoding interpretive boundaries into an auditable consent object before any credential is minted. These are two separable products that may be built by two different players.

The question of whether card networks are positioned to own this is worth taking seriously rather than dismissing. Visa and Mastercard have demonstrated real capability in extending tokenization vaults to carry contextual rules — Visa Flexible Credential is evidence that the networks can encode policy, not just identity. The honest argument against network ownership is not about capability; it is about update latency and incentive alignment. Token vault updates today require scheme API calls that sit outside the real-time policy change workflows of enterprise HR and finance systems. More structurally, networks are intermediaries between issuers and merchants — they are not designed to be the authoritative source of an enterprise's internal spending policy. An employer's travel mandate is not a scheme rule; it is an employer rule, and the liability for enforcing it sits with the employer and their issuer, not with Visa's network operations. The more likely architecture is that issuers — or issuer-adjacent platforms — expose policy subscription APIs that enterprise identity providers (Microsoft Entra, Okta, and the emerging agentic identity layer) can write to in real time, with the network's token vault receiving downstream updates rather than owning the policy state itself. The regulatory pressure on financial institutions to maintain auditable records of agentic authorization — already visible in early central bank guidance on AI in payments — reinforces the issuer as the natural accountability anchor. The build opportunity for startups is in the structured consent representation layer: translating human-language mandates into machine-readable policy objects that issuers can ingest at credential issuance and update against throughout the credential lifecycle.

What Happened (and Why It Matters)

  • Visa Flexible Credential ships contextual spending controls at the token level — allows a single credential to carry MCC restrictions, velocity limits, and time-bound authorization windows. Solves the 'what is this agent allowed to buy' problem at issuance. Does not solve policy changes after issuance or ambiguous mandates that were never machine-encoded. (Source)
  • Mastercard agent identity tokens embed merchant scoping and spending controls — extends tokenization infrastructure toward agentic credentialing with structured authorization parameters. Same boundary applies: rules are encoded at minting, not updated dynamically against live enterprise policy state. (Source)
  • x402 protocol defines HTTP-native machine-to-machine payment primitives — removes the human-facing checkout step from AI-initiated transactions, enabling agents to pay directly within a workflow. Currently a specification with limited live deployment; production readiness at scale has not been demonstrated. (Source)
  • Google AP2 proposes dual-protocol payment architecture for agents — aims to unify payment initiation across agentic workflows. Early-stage; independent evidence of broad developer traction beyond initial GitHub activity is not yet publicly confirmed. (Source)
  • IMF flags agentic payment authorization as an emerging supervisory gap — notes that AI agents acting on delegated financial authority create accountability questions that existing AML and consumer protection frameworks did not anticipate. The note does not distinguish between mandate drift and mandate ambiguity as separate failure modes, which limits its usefulness as a spec input. (Source)

Counter-Consensus

The emerging consensus is that agentic payments need better identity and authentication — and that consensus is right. Scheme-level agent credentials are a genuine advance, and the industry was correct to prioritize getting identity infrastructure in place before worrying about higher-order policy problems. The missing assumption is that identity and authorization are the same problem. They are not. A credential can perfectly authenticate an agent — proving it is who it claims to be, acting within its initial permission envelope — while still authorizing a transaction that violates the current policy of the entity that issued the mandate. The constraint that changes is not 'can we trust the agent's identity' but 'can the payment infrastructure reflect the live state of the human organization's intent.' Scheme controls encode intent at a point in time. Enterprise policy is continuous and mutable. The gap between those two facts is where disputes will accumulate.

Morgan's take (Payments Expert, 15 years in card networks & rails)

The mandate ambiguity problem the article identifies is real, but I'd push the author to look at how corporate card programs have historically approximated a solution: pre-trip authorization workflows where a manager signs off on a structured itinerary before the card is activated for that travel window. That's a crude but live precedent for the 'signed consent object before credential issuance' model. The agentic layer needs to formalize what corporate T&E systems have been doing informally for twenty years — and that framing would make the structured consent argument land harder with a fintech or issuer audience.
