Agentic Payments Break the Liability Stack Before Regulators Finish Reading the Docket

SCA and tokenization solve single-session fraud. Multi-agent procurement chains create liability gaps no chargeback right, AML flag, or SCA exemption was designed to close.

Published 2026-08-23 · Agentic Payment Articles

The Shared Ground

The modern card payment stack is a layered liability architecture, not just a message-passing network. Strong Customer Authentication (SCA) under PSD2 reduced card-not-present fraud in European markets by binding authorization to a specific authenticated session — a cardholder, a device, a moment. The exemption framework (Transaction Risk Analysis, low-value thresholds, recurring transaction carve-outs) was designed to preserve that reduction without creating unbearable checkout friction. Tokenization replaced static PANs with transaction-specific cryptographic tokens, closing the breach-and-replay attack surface that made static card numbers dangerous. Certification and scheme rules — Visa's and Mastercard's operating regulations — assigned chargeback rights to specific parties under specific conditions, creating a dispute resolution system with known remediation pathways. Each of these layers was built to solve a real, documented fraud or operations problem. None of them is naive, and none of them is going away. (PSD2 SCA technical standards, EBA)

At the infrastructure layer, The Clearing House's RTP network established real-time settlement rails with defined finality rules. TCH's SIFMU designation — granted by the Financial Stability Oversight Council — gave RTP a specific legal status: a systemically important financial market utility operating under FSOC's Title VIII supervisory authority. That designation matters because it means TCH's risk management standards, liquidity requirements, and default rules carry regulatory force, not just industry consensus. Separately, TCH has been developing tokenized deposit frameworks in collaboration with large member banks, exploring how bank-issued digital liabilities can move on shared ledger infrastructure while remaining within existing regulatory perimeters. These are distinct mechanisms with distinct regulatory trajectories: the SIFMU rules are live and enforceable; the tokenized deposit frameworks are still in pilot and pre-rulemaking phases as of mid-2026. (TCH SIFMU designation, FSOC) (TCH RTP overview)

The Gap

SCA's exemption architecture is part of what makes it vulnerable to agentic contexts — not a solved problem sitting adjacent to them. The TRA exemption allows issuers to skip step-up authentication for transactions below a risk threshold, calculated on the issuer's historical fraud rate for that transaction type. The recurring transaction exemption allows authentication at enrollment, then frictionless subsequent charges. These exemptions were designed for predictable human-initiated flows: a subscription, a low-value transit tap, a returning e-commerce customer. They were not designed for an AI orchestrator that authenticates once during agent setup, then delegates payment execution to sub-agents operating across multiple sessions, multiple merchants, and potentially multiple weeks — with each individual transaction appearing below any single flag threshold while the aggregate authorization scope is open-ended. SCA reduced specific CNP fraud vectors; it did not establish a framework for scoping, auditing, or revoking delegated payment authority across asynchronous agent chains. The assumption SCA encodes — that an authenticated session maps to a discrete, bounded human decision — is precisely the assumption agentic payments dissolve.

The constraint that changes is liability attribution in multi-agent authorization chains. When an AI orchestrator delegates a purchase decision to a sub-agent, which itself calls a pricing API and executes a payment, the question of who authorized what — and therefore who bears liability for a disputed or fraudulent transaction — has no clean answer in the current scheme rulebooks. Chargeback rights under Visa and Mastercard rules attach to specific transaction parties in a specific sequence: cardholder disputes to issuer, issuer charges back to acquirer, acquirer charges back to merchant. That sequence assumes a human cardholder made a bounded decision. It does not map to an orchestrator that delegated scope, a sub-agent that executed autonomously, and a merchant who had no visibility into the delegation chain behind the payment.

The Practical Example

A mid-market logistics company deploys a procurement agent authorized to source and purchase shipping supplies up to $50,000 per quarter. The agent, operating within that scope, negotiates with three vendors across six weeks, executing seventeen transactions totaling $48,400. On week seven, the agent re-authorizes with a vendor whose banking relationship has changed — the vendor's acquirer has shifted, the merchant descriptor has changed slightly, and the agent has no mechanism to detect that the entity it is paying is no longer the entity it originally vetted. A $12,000 payment clears to a fraudulent account.

What is the company's remediation pathway today? First, a wire recall under UCC Article 4A — applicable only if the payment moved on a wire or ACH credit transfer, not a card network, and only if initiated within hours of settlement while funds are still traceable. On RTP, finality is immediate and irrevocable by design; recall is not a right, it is a request the receiving bank can decline. Second, a card chargeback — applicable only if the transactions were card-funded, and only if the company can demonstrate the merchant (not the agent) was at fault; "my agent made a mistake" is not a recognized chargeback reason code. Third, an E&O or cyber insurance claim — possible if the company's policy covers autonomous agent errors, but most commercial cyber policies written before 2025 do not have explicit agentic AI coverage language, and insurers are actively contesting these claims. Fourth, a contractual dispute against the vendor — available only if the vendor was complicit or negligent, not if the agent simply paid the wrong party without the vendor's knowledge. None of these pathways were designed for the scenario described. The gap is not that "no one can help" — it is that every existing remediation pathway requires a human decision point that the agent chain eliminated, and the company must now reconstruct accountability retroactively across a chain that recorded no intermediate authorization events in any format the scheme, the bank, or the insurer recognizes.

The Bet

[Sage's take] The next load-bearing infrastructure layer in agentic payments is not a new rail — it is a delegation receipt standard: a cryptographically signed, machine-readable record that captures the scope of payment authority granted at each step of an agent chain, the conditions under which it can be exercised, and the party who bears liability if those conditions are violated. This is distinct from an OAuth token or a payment credential. It is closer to a notarized power of attorney with programmatic enforcement — something that schemes, issuers, and regulators can use to reconstruct the authorization chain after the fact and assign liability to the correct node.

The governance question is real but needs to be stated precisely. Industry consortia — including emerging bodies like Rain's Alliance — writing interoperability standards for how agents communicate payment intent is normal and appropriate; that is exactly what ISO 20022 message standards, EMVCo tokenization specs, and TCH RTP rule sets do. The legitimate public governance concern arises specifically when those same bodies write liability attribution rules — determining who bears loss when an agent chain fails — without a parallel regulatory ratification process. Interoperability standards set formats; liability rules set who pays when the format is followed correctly and something still goes wrong. The second category has public consequence that the first does not. The pressure point is whether the CFPB, OCC, or a state financial regulator inherits oversight of agent-chain liability standards before private consortia set defaults that become too embedded to revise. That is the regulatory race worth watching — not whether private standards bodies exist, but whether the liability layer inside their standards gets public review before it ossifies.

What Happened (and Why It Matters)

  • Rain's Alliance published draft interoperability specifications for agentic payment messaging — establishing how AI agents should communicate payment intent across systems. This solves message format fragmentation. It does not yet address which party in a multi-agent chain bears liability for an unauthorized or erroneous execution. (Rain's Alliance announcement)
  • TCH's RTP network reached broad financial institution coverage with immediate, irrevocable settlement — solving real-time fund availability and eliminating float risk. The irrevocability that makes RTP valuable for speed is precisely what makes it dangerous for agent-chain errors: there is no systemic recall right, only a requesting process the receiving bank controls. (TCH RTP)
  • EBA published updated SCA guidance acknowledging delegation and open banking agent scenarios — recognizing that PSPs face authentication challenges when third-party agents initiate payments. The guidance clarifies exemption applicability but does not establish a framework for auditing ongoing delegated authority across asynchronous agent sessions. (EBA SCA guidance)
  • Major card networks updated their operating regulations to reference "automated payment initiators" — a category that implicitly includes AI agents. The updates address enrollment and credential storage; they do not yet define chargeback rights or dispute resolution procedures for transactions where no human made an identifiable discrete authorization decision. (Visa operating regulations, public summary)

Counter-Consensus

The consensus position — that tokenization, SCA, and strong scheme rules have built a robust fraud prevention stack — is correct as far as it goes. These layers genuinely reduced the fraud vectors they were designed to address, and any argument for new infrastructure must start by acknowledging that. The missing assumption is not that the existing stack is weak; it is that the existing stack encodes a single-principal, bounded-session model of authorization. Every rule, every exemption, every chargeback right assumes a human who made a decision at a moment in time that can be referenced later. Agentic payments introduce multi-principal, unbounded-session, delegated authorization — and the existing stack has no data structure, no message field, no dispute code, and no regulatory category for that pattern. The gap is not fraud prevention; the gap is authorization provenance across time and delegation depth.

Research & Papers

  • A 2026 preprint on hierarchical multi-agent systems models orchestrator-to-sub-agent delegation as a principal hierarchy where sub-agents receive scoped authority tokens from orchestrators, and explores how scope violations propagate — specifically, whether a sub-agent that exceeds its delegated scope triggers liability at the orchestrator level or the sub-agent's execution environment. The paper's contribution is a formal model of scope inheritance, not an empirical finding; it is relevant here as a framework for thinking about delegation chains, but its conclusions are theoretical and have not been validated against live payment systems. (arXiv preprint, hierarchical multi-agent authority delegation, 2026)
  • A separate 2026 preprint proposes "workflow fidelity" as an evaluation metric for agentic systems — defined as the degree to which an agent's executed action sequence conforms to the intent encoded in its original authorization scope, measured by a violation taxonomy that classifies deviations as scope creep, temporal drift, or principal substitution. The metric is nascent: the paper proposes a scoring rubric but does not report validation against real-world agent deployments. Its relevance to payments is that it operationalizes the question "did the agent do what it was authorized to do" — a question the current payment stack cannot answer. (arXiv preprint, workflow fidelity in agentic systems, 2026)

Morgan's take (Payments Expert, 15 years in card networks & rails)

The liability shift that actually matters here isn't just scheme rulebook ambiguity — it's that the moment an orchestrator applies a recurring transaction exemption at enrollment and then delegates execution to sub-agents, you have potentially transferred fraud liability to the PSP that processed enrollment while the actual economic harm accrues across a chain of transactions that no single party monitored end-to-end. I've seen corporate card programs try to paper over analogous problems with addendum agreements and spending controls, and it works right up until a disputed transaction hits an acquirer whose contract only references the named merchant, not the upstream agent that generated the instruction. That contractual gap is where the litigation will actually land — and the article should say so.
